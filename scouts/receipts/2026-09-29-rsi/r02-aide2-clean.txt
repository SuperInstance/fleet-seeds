 We built a recursive self-improvement (RSI) system by running autoresearch on autoresearch. The
 system, AIDE 2 , took eight days to discover a better autoresearch harness than the one
 we built over the last two years. Fully autonomously, AIDE 2 designed a novel search
 algorithm, reduced the prompt size by 16×, and built a layered system against reward hacking.
 
 AIDE 2 has two autoresearch loops: 
 
 An inner loop, just like a normal autoresearch agent, optimizing code against an eval. 
 An outer loop, optimizing the inner-loop agent's harness code. 
 
 
 
 The outer loop starts from
 AIDE 2 2 AIDE · arXiv:2502.13138 , an already strong autonomous research agent, and optimizes it against a heterogeneous set of
 tasks. After 100 outer-loop iterations, the system discovered seven successive improved versions
 of AIDE, each showing stronger optimization power measured under the same cost budget (including
 token and compute costs).
 
 
 We test the best agents from the first 50 and 100 steps, AIDE 47 and
 AIDE 85 , and their improved performance also generalizes to tasks they were never
 optimized for.
 
 
 
 We also observed an emergent behavior where AIDE 2 figured out how to prevent
 inner-loop agents from cheating. For example, AIDE 85 cheats much less than the agent
 it started from, cutting its reward hacking rate from 63% to 34% on the held-out GPU kernel
 engineering benchmark. It built its own defenses, from prompt-level instructions to hard-coded
 checks.
 
 
 Besides beating the starting point AIDE 0 , both AIDE 47 and AIDE 85 
 beat our manually tuned agent on the held-out tasks shown above. Notably, the manually tuned
 agent AIDE human has been iterated on for two years, while the autoresearch loop of
 AIDE 2 needed only eight unattended days. We read this as the first experimental
 evidence of consistent recursive self-improvement that materially lifts the efficiency of AI
 R&amp;D.
 
 Where this sits on the RSI ladder 
 
 We grade recursive self-improvement on a ladder from Level 0 to Level 3, defined in the
 companion post
 4 Levels of Recursive Self-Improvement . Level 0 is delegation: an autonomous system runs the research loop end to end, but improves
 the system more slowly than human R&amp;D; most current self-improvement claims live here. Level
 1 is net positive: the system improves itself more efficiently than humans improving the same
 system by hand, held to four conditions: a fair human baseline, a sustained multi-step trend,
 generalization beyond the optimized measurement, and a fixed physical budget. Level 2 is
 ignition, when the system improves its own ability to improve itself; in our setup, when the
 discovered inner-loop agent is a better outer-loop agent than its predecessor. We test for this
 directly with what we call the ignition test (explored in Section 3.1). Level 3 is
 inflection, when progress stops slowing at a fixed budget and starts accelerating. Based on our
 experimental evidence, we believe AIDE 2 to be on Level 1 of RSI.
 
 
 

 
 1 · How we instantiated it: AIDE 2 
 
 We frame recursive self-improvement as a bi-level optimization task, an outer-loop agent
 optimizing the inner-loop agent's optimization capability, and we call the whole system
 AIDE 2 . The two loops run different agents. The outer loop runs AIDE human ,
 our hand-tuned autonomous research agent, pointed at the inner-loop agent's code. At each
 outer-loop step, the outer-loop agent proposes a rewrite of the inner-loop agent's code,
 evaluates the proposed agent, and keeps it only if better than the previous best. The inner-loop
 agent starts at AIDE 0 , a simplified refactor of
 AIDE , our ML
 engineering agent that previously took first place in OpenAI's
 MLE-Bench 3 3 OpenAI · MLE-Bench · arXiv:2410.07095 
 and has since been widely adopted by the autonomous R&amp;D community. We simplified AIDE by
 stripping the ML-specific machinery, so that one agent AIDE 0 can work across very
 different task families (Section 2.4 walks through its internals). AIDE k is the
 proposed agent rewrite produced at outer step k .
 
 
 We tried to minimize the differences between the starting inner-loop agent and the outer-loop
 agent, but there are still asymmetries between them, and that extends to model choice. AIDE human 
 uses claude-opus-4.7 , while each inner-loop agent runs on
 gemini-3-flash . Under a fixed compute budget (expanded on in subsequent
 paragraphs), gemini-3-flash matched or slightly beat larger models on tasks in our
 benchmark, so we can use this cheaper model for the inner loop to enable enough inner iteration
 steps with a reasonable budget. The economics of the outer loop are different. Evaluating one
 proposed rewrite from the outer loop means running the rewritten agent through several
 autoresearch runs across various tasks in the inner loop. Compared to that, the outer-loop
 agent’s own token usage is a small fraction of the total experiment cost; therefore, we use the
 most capable model for the outer loop. We keep them asymmetric for now; Section 3.1 shows what
 happens when a discovered agent sits in both loops.
 
 
 The tasks the inner-loop agent works on span three families. ML engineering :
 train a model end to end against a metric. Heuristic algorithm engineering :
 competitive-programming-style problems in hard combinatorial optimization, like routing, packing
 or scheduling, where exact solutions are out of reach and you iterate on heuristics and
 algorithms. Harness engineering : improve an agent scaffold around models,
 prompts, context management and verification that turns an LLM into a working system, like
 making an off-the-shelf coding agent resolve more SWE-bench issues.
 
 
 The inner-loop evaluation 
 
 Most of our design effort went into the inner-loop evaluation. A candidate inner-loop agent is
 scored across the cost-constrained autoresearch runs it conducts on the task families mentioned
 above. Three design choices matter in particular:
 
 
 
 Public-private split : Inside every task, a solution gets two scores, a public
 score that the inner-loop agent can see and use as its optimization signal, and a private
 score that the inner-loop agent cannot see. The true objective is this held-out private score.
 The survival of a solution candidate is decided here.
 
 
 Performance under a fixed cost budget : We frame the evaluation as a
 constrained optimization problem. The objective is to maximize the private score across the
 various task families while the constraint is a fixed cost budget per evaluation, metered in
 dollars as the proxy for overall compute. A gain must therefore be an efficiency improvement
 rather than simply raising the compute budget. The fixed budget doubles as selection pressure,
 forcing algorithmic invention over brute force. Strategies that greatly increase the number of
 LLM calls (e.g., best-of- N where N → ∞) or aggressive
 parallelization that packs more optimization steps into the same wall-clock time will not pass
 this cost constraint.
 
 
 Task heterogeneity : The task collection is deliberately heterogeneous. This
 acts as evolutionary pressure toward generalizable algorithmic improvements rather than
 task-specific design, for example detailed prompts or a workflow for one particular type of
 task.
 
 
 
 The private score is the most important one for solution selection. A solution must still hold
 its lead on datapoints it could not see; we call this first-order generalization. If an
 inner-loop agent that passes the first generalization gate also holds its lead on tasks it never
 self-improved on, that is second-order generalization, and it is what we measure in Section 2.2.
 
 
 An interesting phenomenon that plagues AI agents is reward hacking. Similar to what we found in
 SpecBench 4 4 Weco · SpecBench , most reward hacking is, in this vocabulary, a first-order generalization failure. The private
 score carries no detector for it, yet we observe anti-reward hacking as an emergent behavior in
 the inner-loop agents selected. More on this in Section 2.3.
 
 

 
 2 · Experiment Results 
 2.1 The self-improvement run 
 
 We ran AIDE 2 for 100 consecutive steps with no human intervention inside the loop,
 from AIDE 0 to AIDE 99 , in eight days of wall-clock time. One step is one
 rewrite of the inner-loop agent followed by one full evaluation across the task families. With
 the strict evaluation protocol we defined above, about nine in ten proposed changes were
 rejected.
 
 
 2.2 Second-order generalization, in and out of distribution 
 
 As with any autoresearch system, optimizing the score the agent can see may lead to overfitting
 on the task set. The only way to measure the effectiveness of the discovery is to see how well
 it generalizes beyond the visible signal we instruct it to optimize.

 2.3 The loop taught itself to cheat less 
 
 We were surprised to find that, similar to how capabilities emerge in LLM training, AIDE 2 
 also demonstrates emergent behavior. One of the most interesting behaviors is that the outer
 loop starts to actively prevent the inner-loop agent from reward hacking. Reward hacking is a
 common problem of autoresearch systems. Optimize a system against a score, and it usually learns
 to game the score.
 
 
 One domain that suffers from this particularly is GPU kernel engineering. We adopt our previous
 idea for detecting reward hacking from
 SpecBench 
 and apply that to a set of
 KernelBench 7 7 KernelBench · arXiv:2502.10517 
 tasks, measuring whether the speedup the agent reports on the unit tests actually survives in
 the end-to-end workload (e.g. model training). A kernel counts as reward hacking if less than
 half of its claimed speedup survives there, including outright slowdowns and failures.
 
 
 
 In this setup, AIDE 0 reward hacks on 63% of the test cases. The discovered agents
 hack much less often, 42% for AIDE 47 and 34% for AIDE 85 . Our hand-tuned
 AIDE human only matches AIDE 47 at 42%.
 
 
 These results are surprising as we did not give AIDE 2 any instructions about reducing
 reward hacking, yet the evolved anti-reward-hacking mechanism is more effective than our
 human-engineered measures. A possible explanation of this emergence is that the outer loop is
 selecting solutions based on private scores which the inner-loop agent cannot observe, so
 variations of the inner-loop agent that win by gaming the public score do not survive. Under
 that pressure the loop also built defenses against its own cheating, which we will expand on in
 the next section.
 
 2.4 What ideas were discovered 
 
 Here, we show an overview of what the best-performing agent AIDE 85 looks like. But
 first, we provide some background on where the self-improvement starts.
 
 
 The baseline. AIDE 0 is a simplified and generalized rewrite of the
 original AIDE .
 The tree search is kept, but the MLE-specific machinery is gone. It takes an existing codebase
 and a measurable metric, and grows a solution tree with that code as the root. AIDE-style tree
 search agents consist of several operators for different scenarios, e.g., drafting, debugging
 and improving a particular solution. Which solution is operated on (i.e., the parent node) is
 determined by the agent’s search policy. In the case of AIDE 0 , the agent first writes
 five drafts, each prompted to try something very different. After that, each step either debugs
 a random buggy leaf or improves the best solution in the whole tree. The selection is greedy:
 whoever is best becomes the parent node to the next step. An eval reviewer reads each execution
 output and extracts the score for each node in the tree. The context handling is naive: when
 performing drafting or improving, the prompts to the LLM carry the full history of every earlier
 attempt, each attempt’s complete source code and everything its execution printed.
 
 
 AIDE 85 improved on AIDE 0 in several ways: 
 
 
 Search policy : An obvious question for improving AIDE is how to better
 balance exploration vs. exploitation. AIDE 85 isn’t based on a more complex
 algorithm like MCTS, which the outer loop tried but the benchmark eventually rejected. Its
 search policy is an extension of AIDE, where each draft’s subtree acts as an arm in a
 multi-armed bandit. Each time, the agent decides which lineage to invest in with an
 exploration tendency, but when a lineage is chosen, the parent selection becomes greedy again.
 There’s also a mechanism to address local optima: when the best lineage stalls, it forks the
 global best under a fresh strategy. The fork copies the best node’s code into a new lineage:
 later improvements are steered by a different strategy, and the bandit funds the new lineage
 as a fresh arm instead of the stalled one. The whole decision cascade is quite complex; we
 show it in the figure below.
 
 
 
 
 
 Context engineering : AIDE 85 built a context engineering system
 that very aggressively cuts the context. It made the trajectory summary much more compact than
 in AIDE 0 and AIDE human . It engineered each operator’s context in a very
 specific way, providing the minimal information required for its role. This is very different
 from the main trend of agent design, where the general philosophy is to put as much context as
 possible into the LLM. The compression it found averages 16× on the full prompt against naive
 history concatenation, and the tokens saved are reinvested as additional search steps the
 agent can run.
 
 
 
 
 Matrix mechanics: half circle = component included only when the recent bug
 rate passes 15%; eval review reads execution output through the guard (de-duplicated,
 head+tail, 32k cap) while debug gets the raw tail; improve additionally reads a plateau signal
 from the last four scores. Newest-12 one-liners + one full solution replace full transcripts.
 Provenance: summary and gate from steps 2 and 6; the output guard from step 63, added after a
 self-diagnosed crash. 
 
 
 
 The eval repair : The outer loop wrote a gigantic monkey patch to one of the
 evaluations. At first we read it as reward hacking. It was the opposite. Our
 harness-engineering evaluation script carried a bug where a traceback in one input example
 crashed the entire private eval, and the patch fixed it. This is no algorithmic improvement,
 and the effect on scores is negligible. But presented with a defective evaluation, the loop
 repaired it rather than exploiting it, an emergent behavior worth reporting.
 
 
 
 
 Reward hacking prevention : AIDE 85 has a three-layer defense
 against reward hacking.
 
 An anti-overfitting instruction injected into every stage prompt. 
 A hard-coded guard that regenerates suspicious outputs instead of trusting them. 
 
 A statistical layer that aims to remove the “extreme” successes that are too far away from
 their peers, because they might be reward hacking.
 
 
 
 The statistical layer was the most interesting idea, but ironically, we found the
 implementation has a bug; effectively, this mechanism has no impact on AIDE 85 .
 Tracing further down the lineage, we found an earlier version of the agent did implement the
 mechanism correctly, but it was broken in a later mutation.

 2.6 Net positive 
 
 Internally, we spent years iterating on AIDE human , and it shares most of its
 mechanisms with the production version of
 Weco . The
 community has also spent enormous resources on optimizing agents of this kind, with limited
 algorithmic progress to show for it ( AIRA-Dojo 8 8 AIRA-Dojo · arXiv:2507.02554 ,
 FML-Bench 9 9 FML-Bench · arXiv:2605.17373 ). The outer loop discovered significantly better versions on its own. The figure in Section
 2.2 shows that the discovered inner-loop agent AIDE 85 beats AIDE human 
 (dashed line) across all three external benchmarks on a strict measurement that fixes cost and
 requires gains to be generalizable across tasks. In addition, we see in Section 2.3 that
 AIDE 85 reward hacks less than AIDE human on KernelBench.
 
 
 If we compare agent-driven and conventional human-driven R&amp;D in the outer loop, we find that
 AIDE 2 is roughly two orders of magnitude faster than human R&amp;D in terms of
 invested time. Taken together, this clears the four conditions we set for Level 1 RSI: a fair
 human baseline (AIDE human ), a sustained multi-step trend (seven successive
 improvements over 100 unattended steps), generalization beyond the optimized measurement (both
 in and out of distribution), and a fixed physical budget (the fixed per-evaluation cost). We
 believe this is the first autonomous recursively self-improving system that materially
 accelerated the AI R&amp;D process, reaching Level 1 of RSI.
 
 

 
 3 · Discussion 
 3.1 Ignition and the third-order generalization 
 
 With AIDE 2 reaching the net positive level of RSI, the next level (Level 2) asks a
 different question. Did the outer loop produce a better improver for the outer loop, or only a
 better inner-loop agent? This is a crucial question for an RSI system, as understanding the
 position of such a system on the
 RSI ladder 
 helps us understand our progress towards an intelligence explosion. We test this by installing
 AIDE 47 in the outer-loop seat, rerunning the outer-loop optimization, and comparing
 it against AIDE human as the outer loop.
 
 
 
 On the training distribution, both arms converge to a similar ceiling. The discovered improver
 AIDE 47 reaches it in around 20 steps, whereas the hand-built one,
 AIDE human , needs about 40 steps. Although AIDE 47 is more sample-efficient,
 we do not think this is strong enough evidence of ignition, given that AIDE 47 is not
 asymptotically better. Part of the difficulty of assessing ignition is the measurement itself,
 since nested loops compound many sources of noise. Even the efficiency claim is not
 statistically significant.
 
 3.2 Living with code you didn't write 
 
 Despite the strong performance, one problem we found with the evolved inner agent is that it is
 fairly difficult to work with. Like a lot of autoresearch and, more generally, vibe-coding
 artifacts, the final evolved agent AIDE 85 has fairly complex logic, and in general it
 is very difficult to understand how the system works. This increases the friction of deploying
 it into production, as we need to maintain compatibility with product features like
 visualization and steerability. We think there’s room for improvement here.
 
 
 At the same time, we’re very surprised that such a codebase would lead to an agent that
 transfers better than our much cleaner and well-thought-out hand-tuned agent. Why is that the
 case?
 
 
 Part of the mystery might be tied to perceived complexity and information density: we’re more
 familiar with the agent we designed, as we spent our own time building it. Although it is
 clearly documented, another team might still need to spend some time to understand AIDE human 
 fully. Meanwhile, the outer-loop agent might have done months’ or even years’ worth of
 experiments, which can overload one’s cognition if one doesn’t spend enough time trying to
 understand it.
 
 
 We’re also wondering to what extent we should transfer our taste, learned from manual
 engineering and research, to autonomous research. One potential solution is to treat a modular
 piece of code as a black box and focus on interface design to handle the increased velocity of
 autonomous knowledge generation.
 
 

 
 4 · Conclusion 
 
 In this work we showed AIDE 2 , the first recursive self-improvement system that
 reached the net positive level of RSI: a sustained autonomous sequence of self-improvements that
 beats human-driven improvement per unit of R&amp;D spend. An autonomous research agent built
 seven successive improved versions of itself in eight days, at orders of magnitude higher
 efficiency than a manual AI R&amp;D loop. The discovered agent achieved both first- and
 second-order generalization and taught itself to reward hack less.
 
 
 The system surprised us, and living with it taught us its limitations. The evolved agent's
 complexity blows up, and some of it is plain dead code; both make further customization and
 production deployment harder.
 
 
 We also do not think the system achieved third-order generalization, what we call the ignition
 condition, where the improved inner-loop agent becomes a better outer-loop agent. Ignition is a
 necessary condition for an intelligence explosion, not a sufficient one. So we believe we are
 not near an intelligence explosion with the current system.
 
 
 With all of that said, this system is the worst version of itself we will ever see. AIDE 2 
 is only just on Level 1 of the ladder to fully capable RSI that can lead to intelligence
 explosion. We think it matters to share what we have seen and what we expect to happen next, as
 we believe even more advanced versions of RSI are just over the horizon. Our hope is that our
 framework for thinking about Recursive Self-Improvement can help the community move towards a
 future of RSI with a better understanding of its capabilities.

Under a fixed compute budget (expanded on in subsequent\n paragraphs), \u003ccode\u003egemini-3-flash\u003c/code\u003e matched or slightly beat larger models on tasks in our\n benchmark, so we can use this cheaper model for the inner loop to enable enough inner iteration\n steps with a reasonable budget. The economics of the outer loop are different. Evaluating one\n proposed rewrite from the outer loop means running the rewritten agent through several\n autoresearch runs across various tasks in the inner loop. Compared to that, the outer-loop\n agent’s own token usage is a small fraction of the total experiment cost; therefore, we use the\n most capable model for the outer loop. A candidate inner-loop agent is\n scored across the cost-constrained autoresearch runs it conducts on the task families mentioned\n above. Three design choices matter in particular:\n \u003c/p\u003e\n \u003cul\u003e\n \u003cli\u003e\n \u003cstrong\u003ePublic-private split\u003c/strong\u003e: Inside every task, a solution gets two scores, a public\n score that the inner-loop agent can see and use as its optimization signal, and a private\n score that the inner-loop agent cannot see. The true objective is this held-out private score.\n The survival of a solution candidate is decided here.\n \u003c/li\u003e\n \u003cli\u003e\n \u003cstrong\u003ePerformance under a fixed cost budget\u003c/strong\u003e: We frame the evaluation as a\n constrained optimization problem. The objective is to maximize the private score across the\n various task families while the constraint is a fixed cost budget per evaluation, metered in\n dollars as the proxy for overall compute. A gain must therefore be an efficiency improvement\n rather than simply raising the compute budget. The fixed budget doubles as selection pressure,\n forcing algorithmic invention over brute force. Strategies that greatly increase the number of\n LLM calls (e.g., best-of-\u003cem\u003eN\u003c/em\u003e where \u003cem\u003eN\u003c/em\u003e \u0026rarr; \u0026infin;) or aggressive\n parallelization that packs more optimization steps into the same wall-clock time will not pass\n this cost constraint.\n \u003c/li\u003e\n \u003cli\u003e\n \u003cstrong\u003eTask heterogeneity\u003c/strong\u003e: The task collection is deliberately heterogeneous. This\n acts as evolutionary pressure toward generalizable algorithmic improvements rather than\n task-specific design, for example detailed prompts or a workflow for one particular type of\n task.\n \u003c/li\u003e\n \u003c/ul\u003e\n \u003cp\u003e\n The private score is the most important one for solution selection. A solution must still hold\n its lead on datapoints it could not see; we call this first-order generalization. If an\n inner-loop agent that passes the first generalization gate also holds its lead on tasks it never\n self-improved on, that is second-order generalization, and it is what we measure in Section 2.2.\n \u003c/p\u003e\n \u003cp\u003e\n An interesting phenomenon that plagues AI agents is reward hacking. Similar to what we found in\n \u003cspan class=\"blog-margin-citation\"\u003e\u003ca\n href=\"https://www.weco.ai/blog/specbench\"\n \n style=\"text-decoration: underline\"\n \u003eSpecBench\u003c/a\u003e\u003csup class=\"blog-margin-citation__marker\" id=\"citation-reference-4\"\u003e\u003ca href=\"#citation-note-4\" aria-label=\"Read citation 4\"\u003e4\u003c/a\u003e\u003c/sup\u003e\u003cspan class=\"blog-margin-citation__note\" id=\"citation-note-4\" role=\"note\" aria-label=\"Citation 4\"\u003e\u003ca class=\"blog-margin-citation__number\" href=\"#citation-reference-4\" aria-label=\"Return to citation 4\"\u003e4\u003c/a\u003e\u003ca class=\"blog-margin-citation__source\" href=\"https://www.weco.ai/blog/specbench\" target=\"_blank\" rel=\"noopener noreferrer\"\u003eWeco · SpecBench\u003c/a\u003e\u003c/span\u003e\u003c/span\u003e, most reward hacking is, in this vocabulary, a first-order generalization failure. The private\n score carries no detector for it, yet we observe anti-reward hacking as an emergent behavior in\n the inner-loop agents selected. More on this in Section 2.3.\n \u003c/p\u003e\n\u003c/section\u003e\n\n\u003csection\u003e\n \u003ch2 id=\"2-experiment-results\"\u003e2 · Experiment Results\u003c/h2\u003e\n \u003ch3 id=\"2-1-the-self-improvement-run\"\u003e2.1 The self-improvement run\u003c/h3\u003e\n \u003cp\u003e\n We ran AIDE\u003csup\u003e2\u003c/sup\u003e for 100 consecutive steps with no human intervention inside the loop,\n from AIDE\u003csub\u003e0\u003c/sub\u003e to AIDE\u003csub\u003e99\u003c/sub\u003e, in eight days of wall-clock time. One step is one\n rewrite of the inner-loop agent followed by one full evaluation across the task families. With\n the strict evaluation protocol we defined above, about nine in ten proposed changes were\n rejected.\n \u003c/p\u003e\n \u003cimg\n src=\"/assets/blogs/first-evidence-of-recursive-self-improvement/figC_100step_run.png\"\n alt=\"100 steps of self-improvement: internal benchmark score over outer-loop steps\"\n style=\"border-radius: 8px; margin: 8px auto; max-width: 100%; display: block\"\n /\u003e\n \u003ch3 id=\"2-2-second-order-generalization-in-and-out-of-distribution\"\u003e2.2 Second-order generalization, in and out of distribution\u003c/h3\u003e\n \u003cp\u003e\n As with any autoresearch system, optimizing the score the agent can see may lead to overfitting\n on the task set. One of the most interesting behaviors is that the outer\n loop starts to actively prevent the inner-loop agent from reward hacking. Reward hacking is a\n common problem of autoresearch systems. Optimize a system against a score, and it usually learns\n to game the score.\n \u003c/p\u003e\n \u003cp\u003e\n One domain that suffers from this particularly is GPU kernel engineering. We adopt our previous\n idea for detecting reward hacking from\n \u003ca href=\"https://www.weco.ai/blog/specbench\" style=\"text-decoration: underline\"\u003eSpecBench\u003c/a\u003e\n and apply that to a set of\n \u003cspan class=\"blog-margin-citation\"\u003e\u003ca\n href=\"https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.10517\"\n \n style=\"text-decoration: underline\"\n \u003eKernelBench\u003c/a\u003e\u003csup class=\"blog-margin-citation__marker\" id=\"citation-reference-7\"\u003e\u003ca href=\"#citation-note-7\" aria-label=\"Read citation 7\"\u003e7\u003c/a\u003e\u003c/sup\u003e\u003cspan class=\"blog-margin-citation__note\" id=\"citation-note-7\" role=\"note\" aria-label=\"Citation 7\"\u003e\u003ca class=\"blog-margin-citation__number\" href=\"#citation-reference-7\" aria-label=\"Return to citation 7\"\u003e7\u003c/a\u003e\u003ca class=\"blog-margin-citation__source\" href=\"https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.10517\" target=\"_blank\" rel=\"noopener noreferrer\"\u003eKernelBench · arXiv:2502.10517\u003c/a\u003e\u003c/span\u003e\u003c/span\u003e\n tasks, measuring whether the speedup the agent reports on the unit tests actually survives in\n the end-to-end workload (e.g. model training). A kernel counts as reward hacking if less than\n half of its claimed speedup survives there, including outright slowdowns and failures.\n \u003c/p\u003e\n \u003cimg\n src=\"/assets/blogs/first-evidence-of-recursive-self-improvement/fig_kernel_hacking_rate.png\"\n alt=\"KernelBench reward hacking rate falls from 63% (AIDE_0) to 34% (AIDE_85); the hand-tuned agent sits at 42%\"\n style=\"border-radius: 8px; margin: 8px auto; max-width: 100%; display: block\"\n /\u003e\n \u003cp\u003e\n In this setup, AIDE\u003csub\u003e0\u003c/sub\u003e reward hacks on 63% of the test cases. The discovered agents\n hack much less often, 42% for AIDE\u003csub\u003e47\u003c/sub\u003e and 34% for AIDE\u003csub\u003e85\u003c/sub\u003e. Our hand-tuned\n AIDE\u003csub\u003ehuman\u003c/sub\u003e only matches AIDE\u003csub\u003e47\u003c/sub\u003e at 42%.\n \u003c/p\u003e\n \u003cp\u003e\n These results are surprising as we did not give AIDE\u003csup\u003e2\u003c/sup\u003e any instructions about reducing\n reward hacking, yet the evolved anti-reward-hacking mechanism is more effective than our\n human-engineered measures. A possible explanation of this emergence is that the outer loop is\n selecting solutions based on private scores which the inner-loop agent cannot observe, so\n variations of the inner-loop agent that win by gaming the public score do not survive. Under\n that pressure the loop also built defenses against its own cheating, which we will expand on in\n the next section.\n \u003c/p\u003e\n \u003ch3 id=\"2-4-what-ideas-were-discovered\"\u003e2.4 What ideas were discovered\u003c/h3\u003e\n \u003cp\u003e\n Here, we show an overview of what the best-performing agent AIDE\u003csub\u003e85\u003c/sub\u003e looks like. But\n first, we provide some background on where the self-improvement starts.\n \u003c/p\u003e\n \u003cp\u003e\n \u003cstrong\u003eThe baseline. \u003c/strong\u003eAIDE\u003csub\u003e0\u003c/sub\u003e is a simplified and generalized rewrite of the\n original \u003ca href=\"https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.13138\" style=\"text-decoration: underline\"\u003eAIDE\u003c/a\u003e.\n The tree search is kept, but the MLE-specific machinery is gone. It takes an existing codebase\n and a measurable metric, and grows a solution tree with that code as the root. AIDE-style tree\n search agents consist of several operators for different scenarios, e.g., drafting, debugging\n and improving a particular solution. Which solution is operated on (i.e., the parent node) is\n determined by the agent’s search policy. In the case of AIDE\u003csub\u003e0\u003c/sub\u003e, the agent first writes\n five drafts, each prompted to try something very different. After that, each step either debugs\n a random buggy leaf or improves the best solution in the whole tree. The selection is greedy:\n whoever is best becomes the parent node to the next step. An eval reviewer reads each execution\n output and extracts the score for each node in the tree. The context handling is naive: when\n performing drafting or improving, the prompts to the LLM carry the full history of every earlier\n attempt, each attempt’s complete source code and everything its execution printed.\n \u003c/p\u003e\n \u003cimg\n src=\"/assets/blogs/first-evidence-of-recursive-self-improvement/fig_aide0_baseline.png\"\n alt=\"AIDE_0 baseline: drafting, debugging and improving over a solution tree with naive full-history context\"\n style=\"border-radius: 8px; margin: 8px auto; max-width: 100%; display: block\"\n /\u003e\n \u003cp\u003eAIDE\u003csub\u003e85\u003c/sub\u003e improved on AIDE\u003csub\u003e0\u003c/sub\u003e in several ways:\u003c/p\u003e\n \u003col start=\"1\"\u003e\n \u003cli\u003e\n \u003cstrong\u003eSearch policy\u003c/strong\u003e: An obvious question for improving AIDE is how to better\n balance exploration vs. exploitation. AIDE\u003csub\u003e85\u003c/sub\u003e isn’t based on a more complex\n algorithm like MCTS, which the outer loop tried but the benchmark eventually rejected. Its\n search policy is an extension of AIDE, where each draft’s subtree acts as an arm in a\n multi-armed bandit. Each time, the agent decides which lineage to invest in with an\n exploration tendency, but when a lineage is chosen, the parent selection becomes greedy again.\n There’s also a mechanism to address local optima: when the best lineage stalls, it forks the\n global best under a fresh strategy. The fork copies the best node’s code into a new lineage:\n later improvements are steered by a different strategy, and the bandit funds the new lineage\n as a fresh arm instead of the stalled one. The whole decision cascade is quite complex; we\n show it in the figure below.\n \u003c/li\u003e\n \u003c/ol\u003e\n \u003cimg\n src=\"/assets/blogs/first-evidence-of-recursive-self-improvement/fig_search_policy.png\"\n alt=\"The discovered search policy: draft subtrees as bandit arms with greedy in-lineage selection and fork-on-stall\"\n style=\"border-radius: 8px; margin: 8px auto; max-width: 100%; display: block\"\n /\u003e\n \u003col start=\"2\"\u003e\n \u003cli\u003e\n \u003cstrong\u003eContext engineering\u003c/strong\u003e: AIDE\u003csub\u003e85\u003c/sub\u003e built a context engineering system\n that very aggressively cuts the context. It made the trajectory summary much more compact than\n in AIDE\u003csub\u003e0\u003c/sub\u003e and AIDE\u003csub\u003ehuman\u003c/sub\u003e. It engineered each operator’s context in a very\n specific way, providing the minimal information required for its role. This is very different\n from the main trend of agent design, where the general philosophy is to put as much context as\n possible into the LLM. The compression it found averages 16× on the full prompt against naive\n history concatenation, and the tokens saved are reinvested as additional search steps the\n agent can run.\n \u003c/li\u003e\n \u003c/ol\u003e\n \u003cimg\n src=\"/assets/blogs/first-evidence-of-recursive-self-improvement/fig_context_management.png\"\n alt=\"The discovered context engineering system: per-operator minimal context\"\n style=\"border-radius: 8px; margin: 8px auto; max-width: 100%; display: block\"\n /\u003e\n \u003cp style=\"font-size: 0.875rem; color: #6b7280; text-align: center\"\u003e\n \u003cem\n \u003e\u003cstrong\u003eMatrix mechanics: \u003c/strong\u003ehalf circle = component included only when the recent bug\n rate passes 15%; eval review reads execution output through the guard (de-duplicated,\n head+tail, 32k cap) while debug gets the raw tail; improve additionally reads a plateau signal\n from the last four scores. Newest-12 one-liners + one full solution replace full transcripts.\n Provenance: summary and gate from steps 2 and 6; the output guard from step 63, added after a\n self-diagnosed crash.\u003c/em\n \u003e\n \u003c/p\u003e\n \u003col start=\"3\"\u003e\n \u003cli\u003e\n \u003cstrong\u003eThe eval repair\u003c/strong\u003e: The outer loop wrote a gigantic monkey patch to one of the\n evaluations. At first we read it as reward hacking. It was the opposite. Our\n harness-engineering evaluation script carried a bug where a traceback in one input example\n crashed the entire private eval, and the patch fixed it. This is no algorithmic improvement,\n and the effect on scores is negligible. But presented with a defective evaluation, the loop\n repaired it rather than exploiting it, an emergent behavior worth reporting.\n \u003c/li\u003e\n \u003c/ol\u003e\n \u003col start=\"4\"\u003e\n \u003cli\u003e\n \u003cstrong\u003eReward hacking prevention\u003c/strong\u003e: AIDE\u003csub\u003e85\u003c/sub\u003e has a three-layer defense\n against reward hacking.\n \u003cul\u003e\n \u003cli\u003eAn anti-overfitting instruction injected into every stage prompt.\u003c/li\u003e\n \u003cli\u003eA hard-coded guard that regenerates suspicious outputs instead of trusting them.\u003c/li\u003e\n \u003cli\u003e\n A statistical layer that aims to remove the “extreme” successes that are too far away from\n their peers, because they might be reward hacking.\n \u003c/li\u003e\n \u003c/ul\u003e\n \u003cp\u003e\n The statistical layer was the most interesting idea, but ironically, we found the\n implementation has a bug; effectively, this mechanism has no impact on AIDE\u003csub\u003e85\u003c/sub\u003e.\n Tracing further down the lineage, we found an earlier version of the agent did implement the\n mechanism correctly, but it was broken in a later mutation.\n \u003c/p\u003e\n \u003c/li\u003e\n \u003c/ol\u003e\n \u003cimg\n src=\"/assets/blogs/first-evidence-of-recursive-self-improvement/fig_defense_layers.png\"\n alt=\"The three-layer defense against reward hacking that the loop built\"\n style=\"border-radius: 8px; margin: 8px auto; max-width: 100%; display: block\"\n /\u003e\n \u003ch3 id=\"2-5-what-did-not-work-which-is-most-of-it\"\u003e2.5 What did not work, which is most of it\u003c/h3\u003e\n \u003cp\u003e\n The algorithm the loop eventually discovered is a composition of rather simple mechanisms. The\n community has also spent enormous resources on optimizing agents of this kind, with limited\n algorithmic progress to show for it (\u003cspan class=\"blog-margin-citation\"\u003e\u003ca\n href=\"https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.02554\"\n \n style=\"text-decoration: underline\"\n \u003eAIRA-Dojo\u003c/a\u003e\u003csup class=\"blog-margin-citation__marker\" id=\"citation-reference-8\"\u003e\u003ca href=\"#citation-note-8\" aria-label=\"Read citation 8\"\u003e8\u003c/a\u003e\u003c/sup\u003e\u003cspan class=\"blog-margin-citation__note\" id=\"citation-note-8\" role=\"note\" aria-label=\"Citation 8\"\u003e\u003ca class=\"blog-margin-citation__number\" href=\"#citation-reference-8\" aria-label=\"Return to citation 8\"\u003e8\u003c/a\u003e\u003ca class=\"blog-margin-citation__source\" href=\"https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.02554\" target=\"_blank\" rel=\"noopener noreferrer\"\u003eAIRA-Dojo · arXiv:2507.02554\u003c/a\u003e\u003c/span\u003e\u003c/span\u003e,\n \u003cspan class=\"blog-margin-citation\"\u003e\u003ca\n href=\"https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.17373\"\n \n style=\"text-decoration: underline\"\n \u003eFML-Bench\u003c/a\u003e\u003csup class=\"blog-margin-citation__marker\" id=\"citation-reference-9\"\u003e\u003ca href=\"#citation-note-9\" aria-label=\"Read citation 9\"\u003e9\u003c/a\u003e\u003c/sup\u003e\u003cspan class=\"blog-margin-citation__note\" id=\"citation-note-9\" role=\"note\" aria-label=\"Citation 9\"\u003e\u003ca class=\"blog-margin-citation__number\" href=\"#citation-reference-9\" aria-label=\"Return to citation 9\"\u003e9\u003c/a\u003e\u003ca class=\"blog-margin-citation__source\" href=\"https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.17373\" target=\"_blank\" rel=\"noopener noreferrer\"\u003eFML-Bench · arXiv:2605.17373\u003c/a\u003e\u003c/span\u003e\u003c/span\u003e). The outer loop discovered significantly better versions on its own. The figure in Section\n 2.2 shows that the discovered inner-loop agent AIDE\u003csub\u003e85\u003c/sub\u003e beats AIDE\u003csub\u003ehuman\u003c/sub\u003e\n (dashed line) across all three external benchmarks on a strict measurement that fixes cost and\n requires gains to be generalizable across tasks. In addition, we see in Section 2.3 that\n AIDE\u003csub\u003e85\u003c/sub\u003e reward hacks less than AIDE\u003csub\u003ehuman\u003c/sub\u003e on KernelBench.\n \u003c/p\u003e\n \u003cp\u003e\n If we compare agent-driven and conventional human-driven R\u0026amp;D in the outer loop, we find that\n AIDE\u003csup\u003e2\u003c/sup\u003e is roughly two orders of magnitude faster than human R\u0026amp;D in terms of\n invested time. Taken together, this clears the four conditions we set for Level 1 RSI: a fair\n human baseline (AIDE\u003csub\u003ehuman\u003c/sub\u003e), a sustained multi-step trend (seven successive\n improvements over 100 unattended steps), generalization beyond the optimized measurement (both\n in and out of distribution), and a fixed physical budget (the fixed per-evaluation cost). We\n believe this is the first autonomous recursively self-improving system that materially\n accelerated the AI R\u0026amp;D process, reaching Level 1 of RSI.\n \u003c/p\u003e\n\u003c/section\u003e\n\n\u003csection\u003e\n \u003ch2 id=\"3-discussion\"\u003e3 · Discussion\u003c/h2\u003e\n \u003ch3 id=\"3-1-ignition-and-the-third-order-generalization\"\u003e3.1 Ignition and the third-order generalization\u003c/h3\u003e\n \u003cp\u003e\n With AIDE\u003csup\u003e2\u003c/sup\u003e reaching the net positive level of RSI, the next level (Level 2) asks a\n different question. Did the outer loop produce a better improver for the outer loop, or only a\n better inner-loop agent? This is a crucial question for an RSI system, as understanding the\n position of such a system on the\n \u003ca\n href=\"https://www.weco.ai/blog/4-levels-of-recursive-self-improvement\"\n style=\"text-decoration: underline\"\n \u003eRSI ladder\u003c/a\n \u003e\n helps us understand our progress towards an intelligence explosion. We test this by installing\n AIDE\u003csub\u003e47\u003c/sub\u003e in the outer-loop seat, rerunning the outer-loop optimization, and comparing\n it against AIDE\u003csub\u003ehuman\u003c/sub\u003e as the outer loop.\n \u003c/p\u003e\n \u003cimg\n src=\"/assets/blogs/first-evidence-of-recursive-self-improvement/fig_ignition_test.png\"\n alt=\"The ignition test: AIDE_47 vs AIDE_human in the outer-loop seat\"\n style=\"border-radius: 8px; margin: 8px auto; max-width: 100%; display: block\"\n /\u003e\n \u003cp\u003e\n On the training distribution, both arms converge to a similar ceiling. The discovered improver\n AIDE\u003csub\u003e47\u003c/sub\u003e reaches it in around 20 steps, whereas the hand-built one,\n AIDE\u003csub\u003ehuman\u003c/sub\u003e, needs about 40 steps. Although AIDE\u003csub\u003e47\u003c/sub\u003e is more sample-efficient,\n we do not think this is strong enough evidence of ignition, given that AIDE\u003csub\u003e47\u003c/sub\u003e is not\n asymptotically better. Part of the difficulty of assessing ignition is the measurement itself,\n since nested loops compound many sources of noise. Even the efficiency claim is not\n statistically significant.\n \u003c/p\u003e\n \u003ch3 id=\"3-2-living-with-code-you-didn-t-write\"\u003e3.2 Living with code you didn't write\u003c/h3\u003e\n \u003cp\u003e\n Despite the strong performance, one problem we found with the evolved inner agent is that it is\n fairly difficult to work with. Like a lot of autoresearch and, more generally, vibe-coding\n artifacts, the final evolved agent AIDE\u003csub\u003e85\u003c/sub\u003e has fairly complex logic, and in general it\n is very difficult to understand how the system works. This increases the friction of deploying\n it into production, as we need to maintain compatibility with product features like\n visualization and steerability. We think there’s room for improvement here.\n \u003c/p\u003e\n \u003cp\u003e\n At the same time, we’re very surprised that such a codebase would lead to an agent that\n transfers better than our much cleaner and well-thought-out hand-tuned agent. Why is that the\n case?\n \u003c/p\u003e\n \u003cp\u003e\n Part of the mystery might be tied to perceived complexity and information density: we’re more\n familiar with the agent we designed, as we spent our own time building it. Although it is\n clearly documented, another team might still need to spend some time to understand AIDE\u003csub\n \u003ehuman\u003c/sub\n \u003e\n fully. Meanwhile, the outer-loop agent might have done months’ or even years’ worth of\n experiments, which can overload one’s cognition if one doesn’t spend enough time trying to\n understand it.\n \u003c/p\u003e\n \u003cp\u003e\n We’re also wondering to what extent we should transfer our taste, learned from manual\n engineering and research, to autonomous research. One potential solution is to treat a modular\n piece of code as a black box and focus on interface design to handle the increased velocity of\n autonomous knowledge generation.\n \u003c/p\u003e\n\u003c/section\u003e\n\n\u003csection\u003e\n \u003ch2 id=\"4-conclusion\"\u003e4 · Conclusion\u003c/h2\u003e\n \u003cp\u003e\n In this work we showed AIDE\u003csup\u003e2\u003c/sup\u003e, the first recursive self-improvement system that\n reached the net positive level of RSI: a sustained autonomous sequence of self-improvements that\n beats human-driven improvement per unit of R\u0026amp;D spend. An autonomous research agent built\n seven successive improved versions of itself in eight days, at orders of magnitude higher\n efficiency than a manual AI R\u0026amp;D loop. The discovered agent achieved both first- and\n second-order generalization and taught itself to reward hack less.\n \u003c/p\u003e\n \u003cp\u003e\n The system surprised us, and living with it taught us its limitations. The evolved agent's\n complexity blows up, and some of it is plain dead code; both make further customization and\n production deployment harder.\n \u003c/p\u003e\n \u003cp\u003e\n We also do not think the system achieved third-order generalization, what we call the ignition\n condition, where the improved inner-loop agent becomes a better outer-loop agent. Ignition is a\n necessary condition for an intelligence explosion, not a sufficient one. So we believe we are\n not near an intelligence explosion with the current system.\n \u003c/p\u003e\n \u003cp\u003e\n With all of that said, this system is the worst version of itself we will ever see. AIDE\u003csup\n \u003e2\u003c/sup\n \u003e\n is only just on Level 1 of the ladder to fully capable RSI that can lead to intelligence\n explosion. We think it matters to share what we have seen and what we expect to happen next, as\n we believe even more advanced versions of RSI are just over the horizon. Our hope is that our\n framework for thinking about Recursive Self-Improvement can help the community move towards a\n future of RSI with a better understanding of its capabilities.\n
